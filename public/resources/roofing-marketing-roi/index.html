Roofing Marketing ROI: The Measurement Chain from Inquiry to Collected Revenue

Roofing marketing ROI compares the completed-job gross profit produced by one tracked cohort with everything spent to acquire it. The working formula is (completed-job gross profit − channel spend − attributable handling/sales cost) ÷ (channel spend + attributable handling/sales cost), using collected revenue minus direct job costs for gross profit, not signed contract value. Cost per lead prices the top of the funnel; cost per booked job prices the outcome.

Updated: 2026-11-26

Marketing reporting becomes more useful when every stage in the measurement chain has one definition and one owner. Start with the inbound event, not with an assumed job value.

The missed revenue calculator is a planning model; this guide explains how to replace assumptions with operating data.

Use a complete measurement chain

Source → unique inquiry → legitimate opportunity → qualified opportunity → booked estimate → completed estimate → signed job → verified revenue

Keep each stage separate. A phone-button click is not necessarily a completed call. A completed call is not necessarily a roofing opportunity. A booked estimate is not earned revenue.

Define the funnel before calculating ROI

  • Unique inquiry: one homeowner need, after deduplicating repeat attempts.
  • Legitimate opportunity: possible new work relevant to the business.
  • Qualified opportunity: fits the agreed service, territory and customer criteria.
  • Booked estimate: a confirmed appointment on the calendar.
  • Completed estimate: the inspection or estimate actually took place, verified in company records.
  • Signed job: contract status verified in company records. When this guide says booked job, it means a signed job, not a booked estimate.
  • Completed job: production is finished and the job status is verified. Check completion and collection between the signed-job and verified-revenue stages.
  • Revenue: label signed contract value, invoiced value and collected revenue separately and use each definition consistently. The profit-based calculation here uses collected revenue from completed jobs.

The scorecard and audit worksheet gives the counting method for your baseline. Keep one CRM record and one owner for each opportunity; those records are what the ROI calculation reads. Use the lead response-time guide to define contacted as a real two-way exchange, not a delivered message, and the follow-up automation guide to track what happens between inquiry and booked estimate.

Use the same deduplicated cohort and the same cost definition for every stage you compare. The table names channel-only and fuller cost views; label a fully loaded cost, including handling, recovery and attributable sales labor, separately from channel-only spend. To compare costs down the funnel, hold the numerator fixed across stages; the worked example below uses all-in acquisition cost throughout. Divide by signed jobs and completed jobs separately and label each result. With zero jobs in a cohort, cost per job is undefined: report zero jobs rather than dividing by zero. Apply the same rule to any stage with no outcomes and to ROI with no acquisition cost.

Customer acquisition cost = attributable sales and marketing cost ÷ verified new customers. The stage-cost detail lives in cost per roofing lead versus cost per booked estimate.

ROI vs ROAS vs cost per lead

  • Cost per lead = channel cost ÷ leads. It prices inquiries; it says nothing about whether they book or buy.
  • ROAS = collected attributable revenue ÷ advertising spend. It measures revenue per ad dollar; it ignores direct job costs and non-ad acquisition costs.
  • Profit-based marketing ROI = (completed-job gross profit − all-in acquisition cost) ÷ all-in acquisition cost. Of these three, only this measure asks whether the channel made money after the roof was built and paid for, after direct job and acquisition costs.

Do not present any of the three as the others. A channel can win on cost per lead and lose on ROI. This ROI definition does not deduct general overhead; use your company's own hurdle to account for it.

Use gross profit, not signed revenue

Signed contract value, gross profit and collected revenue are three different numbers. Gross profit here means collected revenue minus direct job costs: materials, labor, disposal and commissions as your company defines them. Cancellations, supplements, financing, completion and collection timing can change the economics after the sale. Count each cost once: if a commission is already deducted as a direct job cost, do not deduct it again as acquisition cost.

Keep the cohort open until the economic result is known before calling a channel profitable. Compare cost per completed job against gross profit per completed job: the marketing cost has to sit comfortably below the profit it produced. There is no universal hurdle percentage here. Use your company's own written number after considering margin, cancellations, production risk, overhead, cash timing and capacity.

Follow one mature cohort through collection

Judge a channel on one mature cohort: one source and one acquisition window, followed from inquiry through completed job and collection. For example, follow the inquiries acquired during a chosen month until their outcomes are known. Do not compare this month's spend with this month's signed jobs when storm work and insurance waits push signings weeks later. Label an unfinished cohort as provisional and revisit it as completion and collection records mature.

Show where opportunities leak

Break results out by source, business hours, after hours, location, job type and storm versus normal periods. This can reveal a strong ad channel paired with a weak answer rate, or a high inquiry count with poor service-area fit.

Keep those breakouts inside the same cohort. Review missed-call text-back as one permitted first-contact route after a miss and after-hours answering for calls received while the office is closed. Use the answering service versus AI lead recovery comparison to evaluate handling options fairly with the same cost definition.

Context changes the numbers

Storms, seasonality, territory changes, price changes and capacity constraints can make two periods unlike each other. Note those conditions before declaring an improvement.

Illustrative worked example: follow the same cost pool

Illustration only — hypothetical inputs, not a benchmark, a customer result, or a recovery promise. Replace every figure with your own records.

Assume one channel cohort: $6,000 channel spend + $1,000 attributable handling/sales cost = $7,000 all-in acquisition cost. For this arithmetic only, all 100 unique inquiries are reached: 100 unique inquiries → 80 legitimate opportunities → 60 qualified opportunities → 30 confirmed estimates → 8 completed jobs. Every stage cost below uses the same $7,000 fully loaded pool.

The hypothetical 35% gross margin means $72,800 of direct job costs and $4,900 gross profit per completed job before the $875 acquisition cost per completed job. Using $112,000 of revenue in place of $39,200 of gross profit would ignore those direct job costs and overstate the result.

If 25 of the 100 inquiries were never reached, the same $7,000 stays in the pool. Holding the later qualification, booking and completion shares constant for the illustration, fewer outcomes make every downstream stage cost rise. Recovering unanswered calls does not establish that those callers will qualify, book, or buy; add real recovery costs before comparing fully loaded costs.

Attribute recovery without double counting

  • Use one opportunity ID across calls, texts, forms and appointments.
  • Record the original source separately from the recovery channel.
  • Credit a recovered opportunity only once.
  • Keep estimated pipeline distinct from signed or collected revenue.
  • Replace assumptions with verified values as the job progresses.

Frequently asked questions

How do I calculate roofing marketing ROI?

From one mature cohort: (completed-job gross profit − all-in acquisition cost) ÷ all-in acquisition cost, with gross profit from collected revenue minus direct job costs. Keep the cost definition and cohort consistent across channels.

Is cost per lead or cost per booked job the better number?

Cost per booked job for outcomes; cost per lead only prices inquiries. Use stage costs to find the largest drop, then signed-job, profit, and collected-revenue data for downstream decisions.

What costs belong in marketing ROI?

Channel and platform spend at minimum; for a full view, also decide whether to include call handling, lead services, recovery tools, and attributable sales labor — and state the definition so month-to-month comparisons stay consistent.

Why does a cheap lead channel look unprofitable on ROI?

Because unanswered, unqualified, and unconfirmed leads stay in the cost pool, and signed value is not collected profit. Count each stage from your own records and keep the cohort open until collection is known.
