## Day 22 — LinkedIn Founder Post

Storm demand can make a roofing company look fully booked and operationally overwhelmed at the same time.

The phone queue may contain:

- Active leak reports
- Inspection requests
- Existing-customer concerns
- Insurance questions
- Properties outside the service area
- Vendors, solicitations and repeat attempts

Treating every call as the same priority creates confusion. Treating every call as a sales lead inflates the pipeline.

Before the next surge, define:

1. Current service areas and accepted work
2. Capacity and appointment rules for storm mode
3. Who owns urgent human handoffs
4. Approved safety and escalation language
5. Separate routes for prospects and existing customers
6. What happens when calendars or transfers are unavailable

The goal is a usable queue: one record per property need, a clear disposition and an accountable next owner.

Prepare the workflow before the weather creates the demand.

## Day 23 — LinkedIn Company Post

**Post**

A responsible storm-call workflow has five stages:

**Acknowledge**
Identify the roofing company and confirm the inquiry was received.

**Classify**
Separate a new prospect, current customer, vendor or other caller.

**Capture**
Record the property, contact information, reported need, active water entry and relevant timing.

**Check fit**
Confirm service area, work type and current capacity.

**Route**
Offer an approved appointment, callback request, existing-customer notification or human review.

The workflow can prioritize what a caller reports, but it should not diagnose damage, interpret insurance coverage or promise a crew and arrival time unless those details are confirmed.

## Day 25 — Community Response Template

Use when a roofer asks how to handle evening, weekend, leak or storm calls. Adapt the response to the company’s stated coverage and avoid offering legal, insurance, safety or technical roofing advice.

**Template**

I would build the after-hours process around approved routes rather than treating every leak call as emergency dispatch.

For each eligible unanswered call:

1. Acknowledge the inquiry and identify the roofing company
2. Separate a potential new job from an existing-customer issue
3. Collect the property, reported need, service-area fit and preferred next step
4. Use only the company’s approved urgent-call language
5. Offer a real appointment, assigned callback or escalation path

The roofing company should explicitly define what the workflow may not promise. That normally includes a technical diagnosis, insurance outcome, crew dispatch, arrival time or service availability that has not been confirmed.

Possible immediate threats to personal safety belong with the appropriate emergency service under approved language, not in an ordinary sales appointment workflow.

## Day 27 — LinkedIn Company Post

**Post**

Voicemail, live answering, full-time AI reception and missed-call recovery solve different problems.

**Voicemail** records whatever the caller chooses to leave.

**A traditional answering service** provides live-human coverage during agreed periods.

**A generic AI receptionist** can automate a broader front-desk conversation when carefully configured.

**Missed-call recovery** supports the team after an unanswered, busy or after-hours call and focuses on creating an approved next step.

The models are not mutually exclusive. A roofing company might keep staff as the first line, use live answering during selected hours and add a recovery layer when a call still slips through.

Compare options using the same questions:

- When does it engage?
- How are prospects and customers separated?
- What can it qualify or schedule?
- When does a person take over?
- What outcomes can the roofer verify?
- What is the expected total cost at normal and storm volume?

## Day 29 — LinkedIn Founder Post

**Post**

If I were reviewing a roofing company’s missed-call process after 30 days, I would not begin with “revenue recovered.”

I would ask for the evidence chain:

1. How many unique calls met the missed-call trigger?
2. How many received the configured response?
3. How many became two-way conversations?
4. How many represented possible new roofing work?
5. How many fit the service area and job criteria?
6. How many received a confirmed estimate or assigned callback?
7. How many appointments occurred?
8. Which opportunities became signed work in the company’s own records?

This prevents duplicate callers, existing customers, spam and unqualified contacts from being presented as sales wins.

It also reveals the actual bottleneck. The problem could be response, engagement, fit, appointment capacity, no-shows, estimator follow-up or closing.

Revenue recovery should be measured from the first missed call through an outcome the roofing company can verify.
